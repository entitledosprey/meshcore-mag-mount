# MeshCore magnetic roof mount

A magnetically-mounted repeater mast for a pitched steel roof. It sticks on with
neodymium discs, brings the mast back to plumb whatever the roof pitch, carries
an N-type antenna, and takes different repeater hardware by swapping one flat
plate.

Everything is generated by two Onshape **FeatureScript** files. Paste each into
its own Feature Studio — they are standalone and share no code.

| file | Feature Studio gives you | makes |
|---|---|---|
| `meshcoreMagMount.fs` | *MeshCore mag mount* | base, yaw post, head, antenna cap |
| | *MeshCore top plate* | the swappable device plate |
| `meshcoreRibBase.fs` | *MeshCore rib brace* | an alternative base for a ribbed roof |

Supporting docs: **[DFM.md](DFM.md)** for printing, **[CLAUDE.md](CLAUDE.md)**
for the design rationale and the rules that must not be broken.

## Coordinate frame

Origin at the centre of the base underside. **z = 0 is the roof contact plane**,
**+X is downhill** along the fall line, **+Z** is the roof normal at zero tilt.

## How it works

Three rotations are needed to plumb the mast *and* aim the panel. Only two need
hardware.

| what | how | hardware |
|---|---|---|
| align the tilt plane to the fall line | rotate the whole base on the roof | none — it's magnetic |
| plumb the mast | serrated face clutch at the base, **10° steps, 0–30°** | one M6 + captive nyloc |
| aim the panel | index plate at the top of the yaw post, **15° steps** | 3 × M4 |

The azimuth joint has to sit **above** the tilt clutch. At the base it would
rotate about the *roof* normal rather than about vertical, and panel azimuth
would stay coupled to mast tilt.

### The clutch is single-sided

One toothed ear, not two. A face spline only engages along its axis and the
tongue's ridges are wider than the gap between two rigid ears — a double-shear
clutch has no assembly sequence at all unless one side is a separate bolted-on
part. Single-sided costs nothing: the teeth carry the moment across a full
annulus and the M6 only has to clamp.

### The azimuth has no teeth

The post's top flange is an **index plate: 24 clearance holes**, one per
position. The head has 3 inserts at 120°. Slacken the three bolts, turn the head
to any multiple of 15°, do them back up — the inserts always find three of the
24 holes. The rest sit open and drain the joint.

`azSteps` **must divide by** `azBoltN`, or the three bolts cannot all land on
holes at once. That was the original bug: three holes against three inserts, so
the bolts only met every 120° and 21 of the 24 positions were unreachable.

The teeth came out because rotation about the mast axis is the one direction a
symmetric wind cannot load — the panel force and its position vector both lie in
the vertical plane through the axis, so `r × F` has no z-component. What is left
is gust eccentricity, ~1.1 N·m at 90 mph, against 12.6 N·m that three M4s hold
by friction alone. **The clutch keeps its teeth** and must: that joint takes the
full 9.5 N·m overturning moment on a single M6, where friction gives SF 1.5
before creep.

## Bases

| | cross | round | rib brace |
|---|---|---|---|
| for | thin roof sheet | 3/8 in plate | ribbed roof pan |
| magnets | 8 at r = 120 | 8 at r = 60 | 8 at r = 198 |
| size | 260 mm | 152 mm (6 in) | 304 × 336 mm |
| **tips at** | **101 mph** | **103 mph** | **134 mph** |
| file | `meshcoreMagMount.fs` | same, `baseStyle = ROUND` | `meshcoreRibBase.fs` |

All three take the same yaw post, head, cap and plate.

### The rib brace

For a roof pan carrying small raised stiffening ribs, which a flat base rocks
on. **Nothing touches the roof except the magnet feet** — everything else is
lifted `ribClear` (0.250 in) clear, so the ribs pass underneath and their
*pitch* never has to be known. That matters: the pitch cannot be read off a
photo, and a relief channel in the wrong place is worse than none.

The feet are **24 mm across the ribs** and long the other way, so you set the
brace down with X across the ribs and each foot drops into a flat land. Each
corner is a stadium — a bar with a round boss at each end, and each boss *is* a
magnet — centred on the arm's tip so it reads as a flange on the arm.

The clutch ear is **yawed 90°** relative to the other base. The feet being
narrow in X means the ribs run along Y, roof ribs run up-slope, so Y is the fall
line — and tilting toward ±Y needs the clutch axis along X. Unlike the cross
base this one cannot be spun on the roof to fix it: spinning it puts the feet
across the ribs.

`python3 tools/brace.py --rib-pitch 101.6` shows the placement slack for a given
pitch. It clears 3, 4, 6 and 8 in pitches with margin.

## Wind rating — read this first

Tipping governs, not sliding, and not the strength of anything printed.

4 × D82B-N52 are rated 7.97 lb each **on thick clean steel**. A 24 ga painted
roof panel is 0.19× the magnet's own thickness, so it magnetically saturates and
cannot carry the full flux; with the coating that is about **0.45 × 0.92** of
catalogue. A ferrous backing disc behind each magnet gives the bare disc a flux
return path and buys back ~30 %.

On 3/8 in plate there is **no derate at all**, which is the whole reason the
round base can be a fraction of the diameter.

Two things that sound similar and are not:

* **stacking magnets is near-worthless on thin sheet** — the sheet is already
  saturated, so a second magnet on top adds almost nothing;
* **spreading them is worth a lot** — tip resistance is force × lever, and the
  lever is yours to choose. That is the entire argument for the 336 mm brace.

The quoted speeds are where it **tips**, not a rating. Tether it. The cross base
has a tether eye; the round base and the brace do not — pass a line round an arm.

```
python3 tools/loads.py                                  # defaults
python3 tools/loads.py --mag-r 198 --wind 90            # the brace
python3 tools/loads.py --round --substrate 9.53         # round base on plate
```

## Parts

### Printed — ASA or ASA-CF/GF

PLA will sag on a sunny roof. See [DFM.md](DFM.md) for orientation and the one
part that needs support.

Base · yaw post · head · antenna cap · top plate. About 400 g all in.

### Turned — optional

* **Ø12.7 × 3 steel yoke** behind each magnet. Worth ~30 % of the pull.
* **Ø20 × 2 aluminium washer** under the N bulkhead nut, to keep it from
  creeping into hot plastic. Electrically insignificant at that size.
* `sleeveD` exists for a turned bush on the clutch axis. **Leave it at 0** unless
  the bush is in your hand — non-zero bores the axis to the *sleeve*, not the
  bolt.

### Fasteners

| where | fastener |
|---|---|
| clutch axis | M6 × 30 + M6 nyloc, captive in the base ear |
| antenna cap → mast | 3 × M3 × 10 + M3 inserts (Ø5.0 × 6 bores) |
| azimuth clamp | 3 × M4 × 20 + M4 inserts in the head flange (Ø5.8 × 8) |
| top plate → arm | 4 × M4 × 16 **socket head cap** + M4 inserts in the plate |
| device → top plate | 2 × M6 **socket head cap** + nyloc, counterbored flush |

**Insert bores are sized to measured inserts, not catalogue figures** — the
plain (non-knurled) OD of the ones in stock: M3 at 0.195 in → Ø5.0, M4 at
0.227 in → Ø5.8. The M4 bore had been 5.6, which no 5.77 mm insert will enter.
Measure yours; they are all dialog parameters.

Nothing is countersunk. Cap heads throughout.

### Consumables

8 × KJ Magnetics D82B-N52 (Ø12.7 × 3.175), CA or slow epoxy, and a tether.

## Assembly

**1. Magnets into the base.** Each pocket is open at the bottom and sealed at
the top — deliberately no push-out hole, because a hole in an upward-facing
surface drains onto a bare magnet and pools there. Drop the **steel yoke in
first** from underneath, then the magnet, face flush with the base underside.
Check all eight are the same polarity before the glue goes off; mixed polarity
costs holding force. A drop of CA on the pocket wall is enough — the magnet is
in compression against the roof in normal use.

**2. Nyloc into the base.** The clutch nut drops into its hex pocket on the
outboard face of the ear, down the channel between the gussets. It only has to
not spin.

**3. Yaw post to base.** Bring the post's tongue against the single ear along
the axis, teeth into teeth at roughly the angle you want, and run the M6 in from
the open side into the captive nyloc. Leave it loose.

**4. Azimuth nyloc into the yaw post.** It drops in sideways through the slot.

**5. Antenna cap, on the bench.** The N bulkhead installs from underneath and
once the head is assembled the mast bore is blind at both ends — there is no
sequence that gets a connector in, which is why the panel it mounts to is a
**separate part**. Drop the bulkhead through the cap's D-hole (Ø16.6 / 14.0
across the flats; the flats are what stop it rotating), nut on top, then attach
the pigtail underneath with both hands free. About **8.6 mm of thread** is left
for the antenna after the 9 mm cap, nut and washer.

**6. Cap onto the head.** Feed the pigtail down the Ø20 mast bore, out the side
aperture, then three M3 into the inserts in the mast flange.

**7. Head to yaw post.** Heat-set the three M4 inserts into the **underside of
the head's flange**, then run the bolts **up from beneath** through the post's
index plate into them. From below because the device arm sits directly over the
bolt circle — nothing reaches them from above. Heads stay proud underneath,
where fingers reach.

**8. Coax** runs along the open channel on the arm, 16 mm wide so an assembled
SMA passes. The exit ramps up to the arm's surface over the last 16 mm, so
pushing the cable in from the mast end walks its end out without tools.

**9. Device onto the top plate**, two M6 cap screws through the 40 mm pair.
**Do this before step 10** — their heads land on the plate's mating face and
both holes fall inside the arm pad's footprint, so a proud head would hold the
plate off the pad and leave the joint rocking on two bolt heads. The Ø11 × 6.3
counterbores take an M6 cap head flush with 3.7 mm of plate still under it.

There is no lip to rest the enclosure on while you start these; that ledge is
what made the plate a 132 mm spine. Lay the assembly face-down.

**10. Top plate onto the arm pad.** Heat-set four M4 inserts into the plate's
mating face, hold it on the pad, and run four M4 × 16 cap screws **up from
underneath** through the pad's ears into them.

The ears are why this works. The pad is 80 mm wide against an arm that is never
wider than 48, so the bolts sit at y = ±32 with 8 mm of open air between head
and arm — enough for a driver, not just the head. **The plate goes on and comes
off with the repeater still bolted to it.**

Both of the obvious directions are dead ends and both were built first. Bolting
from behind the pad puts the hole in solid arm — the bottoms land 8–14 mm inside
it. Bolting from the plate's front puts the heads under a 191 × 201 mm device
that covers the plate completely.

## Setting it up on the roof

1. Put the base down with the clutch's tilt plane on the fall line. For the rib
   brace, X across the ribs — the feet have to drop into flat lands.
2. Set the tilt so the mast is plumb. 10° steps, 0–30°.
3. Set the azimuth so the panel faces the sun. 15° steps.
4. Tie the tether off.

### Azimuth range

Panel-to-antenna clearance is the binding constraint, at 21.6 mm with the panel
15° off centre. `python3 tools/geometry.py` sweeps tilt 0–30° and azimuth ±60°
against roof, mast, antenna, azimuth flange and base.

## MC-TOP-1 — the swap interface

Fixed, so every future repeater is one flat plate:

* **80 mm square minimum, 10 mm thick**, flat mating face at 45° to horizontal
* **4 × M4 on a 26 × 64 mm pattern, offset 10 mm up-plane** of the pad centre —
  inserts in the plate, plain clearance holes in the pad, bolts from underneath
* the 64 mm spacing is not arbitrary: it is what puts the bolts outboard of the
  arm, which is what makes them reachable with the device fitted
* the 10 mm offset is not arbitrary either — the pad's down-plane end is given
  over to its taper and is only as wide as the arm there
* **anything the device bolts on with must finish flush on the plate's back.**
  That face is the mating face, and the device's own bolt pattern sits inside
  the pad footprint
* no locating rib. Four bolts set orientation and carry the shear

Pad-local **+X is down-plane**, **+Z is the panel normal**. The plate feature is
built in that frame, which is also how it prints.

### Why the plate is 10 mm and not thinner

Two things are bored from its mating face and both are tall: an 8 mm M4 insert
wants 8 + 2, and a flush M6 cap head wants 6.0 + 2.5. The cap head alone floors
it at 8.5 mm. 0.250 in is reachable only with M4 × 4 short inserts *and* a
button-head M6 — set `insertDepth` 4, `holeCbDep` 3.6.

## Verification

None of this needs Onshape. Run it before trusting any dimensional change; each
exits non-zero on failure.

```
python3 tools/audit_fs.py meshcoreMagMount.fs    # static .fs audit
python3 tools/audit_fs.py meshcoreRibBase.fs
python3 tools/dims.py                            # every primitive's extents
python3 tools/dims.py meshcoreRibBase.fs
python3 tools/preflight.py                       # geometric assertions
python3 tools/brace.py                           # the rib brace
python3 tools/loads.py                           # magnets vs gravity and wind
python3 tools/geometry.py                        # clearances across the range
python3 tools/arm.py                             # arm bending, station by station
python3 tools/docs.py                            # these docs vs the .fs
```

Each tool carries a copy of the defaults it needs and **checks it against the
`.fs`**, so a changed default fails loudly instead of silently drifting. That
has bitten four times; see [CLAUDE.md](CLAUDE.md).

## Before you print

Three numbers are parameterised because they were never measured:

1. **P1-Pro mass.** Everything above assumes 1.3 kg. Every wind number scales
   with it — weigh it.
2. **The enclosure's rib height.** `stripProud` defaults to **0** for that
   reason: a flat plate always works, a raised contact strip only helps once the
   rib is measured.
3. **Your heat-set inserts.** The bores are set to the ones in stock. Measure
   the plain OD before printing.

Print the clutch pair first as a standalone fit test. Tooth engagement and the
M6 fit are the only things that can quietly be wrong.
